Part 1: Introduction to Vacuum Physics

Why Vacuum Physics Matters

Understanding gas behavior at reduced pressures is essential for nanotechnology. While Module 4 introduced vacuum systems and components, this module explores the why behind vacuum technology—the fundamental physics that dictates system design and process parameters.

🔬 The Invisible World of Molecules

At atmospheric pressure (760 Torr), approximately 2.7 × 1019 molecules/cm³ fill every cubic centimeter of air. This creates constant molecular collisions that interfere with nanoscale processes.

At high vacuum (10−6 Torr), density drops to 3.5 × 1010 molecules/cm³—a reduction of 9 orders of magnitude. This dramatic reduction enables precision semiconductor manufacturing.

Kinetic Molecular Theory Review

The Kinetic Molecular TheoryDeveloped by Rudolf Clausius in 1857. Describes gas behavior based on the motion of individual molecules in constant random motion with elastic collisions. (KMT) provides the foundation for vacuum physics:

Random Motion

Gas molecules move randomly with velocities described by the Maxwell-Boltzmann distributionA statistical distribution of molecular speeds. At room temperature, air molecules average ~450 m/s (~1,000 mph).. At 20°C, air molecules average ~450 m/s.

Elastic Collisions

Collisions between molecules and container walls are perfectly elastic—no kinetic energy is lost. This holds well for most vacuum applications.

Point Masses

Molecules are treated as point masses with negligible volume compared to the space between them. Interactions occur only during collisions.

🏭 Industry Connection

Modern semiconductor fabs operate across 15 orders of magnitude in pressure, from atmospheric CVD to ultra-high vacuum surface analysis. Understanding vacuum physics is essential for specifying system requirements, troubleshooting anomalies, and optimizing process conditions.

Part 3: Mean Free Path — The Heart of Vacuum Science

The mean free pathAverage distance (λ) a gas molecule travels between collisions. Inversely proportional to pressure. At 760 Torr: 68 nm. At 10⁻⁶ Torr: 50 m. (λ) determines whether deposited atoms travel from source to substrate without collision, whether flow is viscous or molecular, and what pressures are needed for processes.

Figure 5.1: As pressure decreases, molecular density drops and mean free path increases dramatically.

Rigorous Mean Free Path Equation

λ = kBT / (√2 · π · d² · P)
  • λ = mean free path (m)
  • kB = Boltzmann constant (1.38 × 10−23 J/K)
  • T = temperature (K); d = molecular diameter (for N2: 3.7 × 10−10 m)
  • P = pressure (Pa)

Simplified Engineering Equation (air, 20°C)

λ (cm) = 5 × 10−3 / P (Torr)

This inverse relationship is fundamental. Examples:

  • 760 Torr: λ = 66 nm — atmospheric, constant collisions
  • 10−3 Torr: λ = 5 cm — comparable to chamber dimensions
  • 10−6 Torr: λ = 50 m — far exceeds any chamber

Why MFP Matters for Thin Film Deposition

In thermal evaporationA PVD process: source material is heated until it evaporates, then vapor atoms travel through vacuum and condense on a substrate to form a thin film., atoms must travel from source to substrate. If MFP is shorter than this distance, atoms collide with residual gas, causing:

  • Energy loss: Scattered atoms have less kinetic energy
  • Direction changes: Non-uniform film coverage
  • Contamination: Reactions with gas molecules
  • Poor adhesion: Degraded film quality

📜 Design Rule of Thumb

MFP should be at least 10× the source-to-substrate distance. For a 50 cm chamber, this requires pressures below 10−4 Torr.

Part 4: Vapor Pressure & Clausius-Clapeyron

Vapor pressurePressure exerted by a vapor in thermodynamic equilibrium with its condensed phase at a given temperature. Increases exponentially with temperature. determines when materials evaporate for deposition and which materials are suitable inside vacuum chambers.

Clausius-Clapeyron Equation

ln(P) = −ΔHvap/(R · T) + C
  • P = vapor pressure; ΔHvap = enthalpy of vaporization (J/mol)
  • R = 8.314 J/mol·K; T = temperature (K); C = material constant

Two-Point Form (Practical Use)

ln(P2/P1) = −ΔHvap/R × (1/T2 − 1/T1)

Calculate vapor pressure at one temperature given the value at another—essential for predicting evaporation behavior.

💡 Water Boiling Under Vacuum

Water boils at 100°C at 760 Torr, but at only ~20°C at ~17.5 Torr. Liquids left in vacuum chambers evaporate rapidly and can overwhelm the pumping system.

Material Selection for Vacuum

❌ Avoid in Vacuum

  • Zinc, cadmium (high vapor pressure metals)
  • Most plastics & elastomers
  • Standard lubricants & greases
  • Lead-containing solders
  • Organic materials

✔ Suitable for Vacuum

  • Stainless steel, aluminum, copper
  • Viton, Kalrez (fluoroelastomers)
  • Ceramic, glass, quartz
  • Vacuum-grade greases (Apiezon, Krytox)
  • PTFE (with limitations)
Part 5: Outgassing — The Hidden Gas Load

OutgassingRelease of gas from materials under vacuum. Often the dominant gas load in HV/UHV systems, determining ultimate achievable pressure. is often the dominant gas load in high and ultra-high vacuum systems, setting the ultimate achievable pressure.

Four Sources

  1. Vaporization: Direct evaporation from surfaces
  2. Desorption: Release of adsorbed molecules
  3. Diffusion: Dissolved gas migrating to surface
  4. Permeation: Gas penetrating through walls

Primary Species

  • H2O: Dominant in unbaked systems
  • H2: Dominant in baked metal systems
  • CO, CO2: From organic contamination
  • Hydrocarbons: From oils, greases, fingerprints

⚠️ Never Touch Vacuum Components!

A single fingerprint has an outgassing rate 10,000× higher than clean stainless steel. Always wear clean, lint-free gloves.

Bakeout Procedures

BakeoutHeating a vacuum system while pumping to accelerate outgassing and desorption. Typical: 120–450°C depending on materials and target pressure. is the most effective method for reducing outgassing:

Typical Parameters

  • Temperature: 120–150°C for moderate UHV
  • Duration: 24–72 hours
  • Ramp rate: 1–2°C/min (avoid thermal shock)
  • Higher temp (300–450°C): For extreme UHV

Limitations

  • Elastomer seals limited to ~150°C
  • Aluminum limited to ~200°C
  • Some instruments cannot tolerate heat
  • Significant energy & time costs

💡 Time Dependence of Outgassing

Unbaked water outgassing follows t−1 decay: reducing pressure by another decade requires 10× longer pumping. Baked systems (hydrogen-dominated) follow a more favorable t−1/2 decay.

Gas Flow Regimes & Conductance

The Knudsen Number

The Knudsen numberKn = λ/D. Dimensionless ratio comparing mean free path to characteristic dimension. Determines gas flow regime. classifies three distinct flow regimes:

Knudsen Number

Kn = λ / D
  • Kn < 0.01 → Viscous Flow (molecule-molecule collisions dominate)
  • 0.01 < Kn < 1 → Transition Flow (both types important)
  • Kn > 1 → Molecular Flow (wall collisions dominate)

Conductance Equations

Long Tube Conductance

Viscous (air, 20°C):

C = 180 × D⁴ × Pavg / L

Molecular (air, 20°C):

C = 12.1 × D³ / L

C in L/s; D and L in cm; P in Torr

Effective Pumping Speed

1/Seff = 1/Spump + 1/C

Like resistors in parallel. A 1000 L/s pump through a 100 L/s pipe yields only ~91 L/s at the chamber!

🏭 Design Rule: Short and Fat Pipes

In molecular flow, doubling diameter increases conductance 8× (D³), while doubling length only halves it.

Lab Activity: Vacuum Concepts Exploration

🎯 Lab Objective

Perform mean free path experiments using the Kurt J. Lesker vacuum trainer, observe vapor pressure demonstrations, measure pressure gradients, and compare experimental results with theoretical calculations.

Duration: ~1 hour  |  Equipment: KJL Vacuum Trainer, lab glassware, lighter, water, salt, incense

⚠️ Safety Reminders

Wear safety glasses and lab coat at all times. Handle glassware with care—vacuum flasks can implode. Never vent rapidly. Follow all instructor guidance.

Experiment 1: Incense in a Jar (Mean Free Path)

Place incense into a beaker inside the bell jar. Light it and replace the lid.
Allow the bell jar to fill completely with smoke until opaque.
Close the vent valve. Turn on the pump and open the butterfly valve.
Observe the clearing of smoke. Record: Time to clear and pressure at that point.
Calculate MFP at the final pressure using λ = 5×10−3/P. Compare to bell jar diameter.

Experiment 2: Boiling Water Under Vacuum

Place a small beaker of room-temperature water inside the bell jar.
Begin evacuating while watching the water closely.
Record the pressure at which boiling begins. Compare to theoretical ~17.5 Torr at 20°C.
Repeat with salt water. Observe any difference in boiling pressure.

Experiment 3: Pressure Gradient Measurement

At base pressure, record gauge readings at different system points.
Calculate theoretical conductance of each pipe section.
Compare measured pressure differences with conductance predictions.

Pre-Lab Checklist

Discussion Questions

As pressure decreases, the pump removes gas molecules. With fewer molecules, smoke particles are carried toward the pump inlet rather than remaining suspended. The mean free path of suspended particles increases dramatically.
Dissolved salt lowers water's vapor pressure via Raoult's Law. Since boiling occurs when vapor pressure equals external pressure, salt water requires lower external pressure to boil—demonstrating the colligative property of vapor pressure depression.
Systematic approach: (1) Real leaks—check O-rings, fittings, welds; (2) Virtual leaks—trapped volumes; (3) Outgassing—more pump time or bakeout; (4) Gauge issues—verify calibration; (5) Pump problems—oil level, foreline pressure, turbo speed.
