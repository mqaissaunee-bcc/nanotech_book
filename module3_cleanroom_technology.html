Part 1: Why Cleanrooms Matter

The Nanoscale Challenge

In semiconductor fabrication, pharmaceuticals, and nanotechnology research, cleanroomA controlled environment using HEPA/ULPA filtration, positive pressure, and strict protocols to minimize airborne particulates to specified levels. Temperature, humidity, and pressure are also precisely controlled. environments are not just helpful—they are absolutely essential. Modern microchips contain billions of transistors, each measured in nanometers, where a single particle of dust can be catastrophic.

🔍 The Scale Problem

Consider this: Modern transistors are manufactured at the 3-7 nanometer scale. A human hair is approximately 70,000 nanometers wide. A single dust particle (typically 1-10 micrometers, or 1,000-10,000 nanometers) landing on a microchip during fabrication could completely destroy circuit pathways, rendering an entire wafer of chips worthless.

Scale analogy: If a transistor were the size of a tennis ball, a typical dust particle would be the size of a two-story building crashing onto it!

Sources of Contamination

Understanding contamination sources is essential for effective control. The human body is, by far, the single greatest source of particles in a cleanroom environment.

🧑 Human Sources (75-80% of all contamination)

  • Skin cells: 30,000-40,000 shed per minute
  • Hair: 50-100 strands lost daily
  • Respiratory droplets: Up to 40,000 per sneeze
  • Cosmetics, lotions: Volatile organics
  • Movement: 5-7 million particles/min while walking
  • Talking: 50+ particles per word spoken

⚙️ Non-Human Sources

  • Equipment: Motors, bearings, moving parts
  • Process chemicals: Outgassing and residues
  • Outside air: Infiltration through gaps
  • Building materials: Deterioration over time
  • Supplies: Paper, packaging, cardboard
  • Static discharge: ESD can damage circuits

Part 2: HEPA and ULPA Filtration Technology

Filter Types and Efficiency

HEPA filtersHigh-Efficiency Particulate Air filters that remove at least 99.97% of particles 0.3 micrometers or larger. Originally developed during the Manhattan Project to capture radioactive particles. (High-Efficiency Particulate Air) are the backbone of cleanroom technology. They consist of randomly arranged fiberglass fibers (0.5-2.0 µm diameter) that capture particles through multiple mechanisms.

📜 Historical Note: Manhattan Project Origins

HEPA filters were developed during World War II as part of the Manhattan Project to capture radioactive particles. The technology remained classified until 1950, after which it was adapted for cleanroom use in the emerging semiconductor industry.

Four Capture Mechanisms

HEPA filters capture particles through four distinct mechanisms, each optimized for different particle sizes:

1. Interception

Particles following air streams come within one radius of a fiber and adhere due to van der Waals forces.

Best for: 0.1-1 µm particles

2. Impaction

Larger particles have too much inertia to follow air curves around fibers and collide directly.

Best for: >1 µm particles

3. Diffusion

Tiny particles move erratically via Brownian motion (random collisions with gas molecules) and contact fibers.

Best for: <0.1 µm particles

4. Electrostatic

Charged fibers attract particles of opposite charge, enhancing capture across all sizes.

Best for: All particle sizes

🎯 The 0.3 Micron Myth

Many people incorrectly believe HEPA filters only capture particles ≥0.3 µm. In reality, 0.3 µm is the Most Penetrating Particle Size (MPPS)—the size that is hardest to capture because it falls in the transition zone between diffusion and interception/impaction mechanisms.

Particles both larger AND smaller than 0.3 µm are actually captured MORE efficiently! This is why 99.97% efficiency at 0.3 µm means even higher efficiency for all other particle sizes.

Part 4: Airflow Patterns and Air Change Rates

Unidirectional vs. Non-Unidirectional Flow

➜ Unidirectional (Laminar) Flow

  • Parallel airstreams at 0.3-0.5 m/s
  • Usually top-down (vertical) or horizontal
  • Particles carried directly to return vents
  • Used for ISO 1-5 cleanrooms
  • Requires ~100% ceiling HEPA coverage
  • Higher cost, better contamination control

Best for: Critical semiconductor processes, pharmaceutical sterile filling

🔄 Non-Unidirectional (Turbulent) Flow

  • Mixed airflow patterns
  • Lower air velocity
  • HEPA filters in ceiling with multiple returns
  • Used for ISO 6-8 cleanrooms
  • Partial ceiling HEPA coverage
  • Lower cost, easier implementation

Best for: Medical devices, general manufacturing, teaching labs

💡 Modern Semiconductor Fab Design

Modern fabs use a "raised floor" design with the cleanroom above a sub-fab utility level. Air flows from ceiling-mounted Fan Filter Units (FFUs) down through the cleanroom, then through perforated floor tiles into the sub-fab where pumps, power supplies, and gas lines are housed. This keeps heat, vibration, and maintenance out of the pristine cleanroom "ballroom."

Minienvironments: Wafers travel in sealed pods (FOUPs) and are only exposed to filtered ISO Class 1 air inside process tools, even when the surrounding room is ISO 5.

Air Change Rates

Air changes per hour (ACH) indicates how many times the entire volume of air in a cleanroom is replaced in one hour. Higher ISO classes require dramatically more air changes.

⚡ Energy Considerations

Cleanrooms are extremely energy-intensive. The HVAC system alone can account for 40-60% of total facility energy consumption. Modern facilities use variable-speed drives, low-pressure-drop diffusers, and predictive HVAC maintenance to reduce costs. A typical semiconductor fab can consume 50-100 megawatts of power—equivalent to a small city!

Part 5: Gowning Procedures and Personnel Protocols

The Importance of Proper Gowning

Since humans are the primary source of contamination (75-80%), proper gowning is critical. The gowning sequence is designed to progressively cover contamination sources while preventing transfer to clean garments.

⚠️ Critical Rule

Always gown from cleanest to dirtiest areas. Never touch your face, hair, or exposed skin after gloving. If you do, you must re-glove before continuing.

💡 Gowning Tips

  • Never wear jewelry, watches, or cosmetics in cleanrooms
  • Never use powdered gloves (talc generates particles)
  • Always tuck hood into coverall collar
  • Always double-glove in ISO 5 or cleaner environments
  • Always step on sticky mats or through air showers when transitioning
  • Move slowly and deliberately—rapid movements generate more particles

Cleanroom Behavioral Protocols

❌ Never Do This

  • Run or make sudden movements
  • Touch your face, hair, or skin
  • Bring paper, cardboard, or wood products
  • Wear makeup, perfume, or cologne
  • Eat, drink, or chew gum
  • Write with pencils (graphite particles)
  • Lean on or touch equipment unnecessarily

✅ Always Do This

  • Move slowly and deliberately
  • Keep hands away from face
  • Use cleanroom-approved paper and pens
  • Report any tears in gowning immediately
  • Use designated cleanroom chairs
  • Follow traffic flow patterns
  • Report any spills or contamination events

Hands-On Lab: Build a Mini-Cleanroom

⚠️ Safety Disclosure

This lab uses small particles (sawdust or powder) and a flashlight for visualization. Wear safety goggles to protect eyes. Do not inhale fine particles. Work in a well-ventilated area. Adult supervision required for younger students.

Experiment Objective

Observe airborne particulates and demonstrate the need for cleanrooms through visualization experiments. Students will construct a simulation of a "unit of air" to visualize dust, then build a mockup mini-cleanroom to observe filtration in action.

Procedure: Part A - Visualizing Particles

Prepare the Observation Chamber

Cut a viewing window on one side of the cardboard box. Cover the window with clear plastic wrap, sealing edges with duct tape. Cut a small hole on the opposite side for the flashlight.

Seal the Box

Seal all seams and corners with duct tape to create a relatively airtight chamber. Leave a small flap for introducing particles.

Introduce Particles

In a dimmed room, carefully sprinkle a small amount of sawdust or powder into the box. Seal the flap immediately.

Observe Particle Behavior

Insert the flashlight beam through the hole (perpendicular to viewing window). Observe how particles become visible in the light beam. Note how they float, settle, and move with air currents.

Record Observations

Sketch what you observe. Note how long particles remain airborne. Try gently shaking the box and observing changes.

Procedure: Part B - Mini-Cleanroom

Prepare the Fan Assembly

Cut a hole in the lid of the larger box sized to fit your computer fan. Mount the fan blowing downward into the box. Attach a coffee filter or HEPA filter material over the intake side of the fan.

Create the "Cleanroom" Chamber

Place the smaller box inside the larger box, creating a gap around the edges for air return. Cut small holes in the bottom of the smaller box for air to flow through.

Add Viewing Windows

Cut and cover viewing windows on the sides of the outer box. Insert flashlight hole for observation.

Test the Mini-Cleanroom

Introduce particles into the chamber. Turn on the fan and observe how particles are captured or pushed out. Compare visibility before and after fan activation.

Document Results

Record observations in the data table below. Photograph or sketch the particle distribution with fan on vs. off.
