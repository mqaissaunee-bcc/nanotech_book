Part 1: Why Vacuum Matters in Nanotechnology

Vacuum environments are the foundation of nearly all nanofabrication processes. From thin film deposition to plasma etching, vacuum provides the controlled atmosphere necessary for manipulating matter at the atomic scale. Understanding vacuum technology is essential for any nanotechnology practitioner.

The Three Critical Functions of Vacuum

🛡 Contamination Control

At atmospheric pressure, there are approximately 2.5 × 1019 molecules per cm³. By reducing pressure, we dramatically reduce the number of potential contaminants that can interfere with nanoscale processes.

🔨 Extended Mean Free Path

The mean free path—the distance molecules travel before collision—is extended as pressure decreases. This enables precise control during material deposition. (Physics covered in Module 5)

⚡ Enabling New Physics

Many quantum effects and analytical techniques only work in vacuum, where particles can be controlled without interference from air molecules. This enables electron microscopy, ion implantation, and surface analysis.

💡 Key Concept: Pressure Units

Vacuum pressures are commonly expressed in several units:

  • Torr – 1 Torr = 1 mmHg (most common in US)
  • millibar (mbar) – 1 mbar ≈ 0.75 Torr (common in Europe)
  • Pascal (Pa) – SI unit; 1 Torr = 133.3 Pa
  • Atmosphere (atm) – 1 atm = 760 Torr

Detailed conversions and calculations are covered in Module 5.

Part 2: Vacuum System Overview

A complete vacuum system consists of several integrated components working together. Understanding each component's role is essential for proper system operation and troubleshooting.

Essential System Components

📦 Chamber

The sealed vessel where processes occur. Materials include stainless steel (304/316L), aluminum, or glass.

⚙ Pumps

Create and maintain vacuum. Usually requires multiple pump types working together (roughing + high vacuum).

📈 Gauges

Measure pressure. Different gauge types cover different pressure ranges with varying accuracy.

🔧 Fittings

Connect components leak-tight. Standards include KF (quick), CF (UHV), and VCR (gas delivery).

🚪 Valves

  • Gate valves: Isolate chamber from pumps
  • Vent valves: Controlled air admission
  • Throttle valves: Pressure control
  • Leak valves: Fine gas metering

🔌 Feedthroughs

  • Electrical: Power and signals into chamber
  • Motion: Rotary and linear manipulation
  • Gas: Process gas introduction
  • Optical: Windows for viewing/lasers

🏭 Industry Insight: Pump Staging

No single pump can efficiently cover the full range from atmosphere to high vacuum. Industrial systems use staged pumping:

  1. Roughing pump brings system from 760 Torr to ~10-2 Torr
  2. High vacuum pump takes over at "crossover pressure" (~50 mTorr)
  3. Roughing pump continues as backing pump for turbo/diffusion pumps

⚠ Critical Safety Rules

  • Never vent a turbomolecular pump at full speed – catastrophic rotor failure
  • Never operate ion gauges above 10-3 Torr – filament burnout
  • Always wear safety glasses when working with vacuum systems
  • Use proper lifting techniques – vacuum chambers are heavy
  • Watch for pinch points when making/breaking connections
Part 3: System Bakeout & Maintenance

Achieving and maintaining good vacuum requires proper procedures and regular maintenance.

Bakeout Procedures

Water vapor is the most common contaminant limiting vacuum performance. Bakeout—heating the system while pumping—accelerates water desorption:

🔥 Typical Bakeout Parameters

  • Temperature: 150–250°C
  • Duration: 24–48 hours
  • Ramp rate: 1–2°C/minute
  • Required for: UHV systems

⚠ Bakeout Precautions

  • Remove all elastomer O-rings (max ~150°C)
  • Use metal gaskets (copper, aluminum)
  • Cover with heating tapes/blankets uniformly
  • Monitor all thermocouples during process

Vacuum Pump Technologies

Different pump types use different physical principles to move gas molecules. Understanding each type helps you select the right pump and troubleshoot problems.

🔀 Gas Transfer Pumps

Move gas from inlet to exhaust:

  • Positive displacement: Rotary vane, scroll, diaphragm
  • Momentum transfer: Turbomolecular, diffusion

❄ Capture Pumps

Trap gas molecules permanently:

  • Cryopumps: Freeze gas on cold surfaces
  • Ion pumps: Ionize and bury in titanium
  • Getters: Chemical reaction with reactive metals

⚠ Turbomolecular Pump Safety

A turbo pump rotor spins at 20,000–90,000 RPM. If air suddenly enters:

  • Catastrophic failure – Blades can shatter, bearings destroyed instantly
  • Shrapnel hazard – Metal fragments can penetrate pump housing
  • Always wait for full spin-down before venting (2–10 minutes)
  • Use interlocks – Never rely on manual procedures alone
Pressure Measurement Technologies

No single gauge covers the entire vacuum range. Understanding gauge principles helps you select the right one and interpret readings correctly.

📏 Direct Measurement Gauges

Measure actual force from gas molecules:

  • Bourdon tube (mechanical)
  • Capacitance manometer (diaphragm)
  • Spinning rotor gauge

Advantage: Gas-independent readings

💡 Indirect Measurement Gauges

Measure properties that vary with pressure:

  • Thermocouple / Pirani (thermal)
  • Hot cathode ion gauge
  • Cold cathode ion gauge

Note: Gas-dependent readings

KF Fitting Assembly Procedure

1

Inspect Components

Check flanges for scratches, dents, or contamination. Inspect O-ring for cuts, cracks, or embedded particles. Ensure centering ring is not bent.

2

Seat O-Ring in Centering Ring

Place O-ring into the groove of the centering ring. It should sit flat without twisting. Apply a very thin layer of vacuum grease if specified.

3

Align Flanges

Place centering ring between the two flanges. The O-ring should contact both sealing surfaces evenly. Components should align without forcing.

4

Install Clamp

Slide the clamp over both flanges. For hinged clamps, close and secure the toggle. For wing-nut clamps, hand-tighten until snug—do not over-tighten.

5

Verify Freedom of Movement

The connection should allow slight rotation. If frozen, the O-ring may be pinched. Disassemble and inspect if needed.

⚠ Common Fitting Mistakes

  • Over-tightening: Can crush O-rings or deform copper gaskets
  • Dirty sealing surfaces: Particles create leak paths
  • Reusing copper gaskets: CF gaskets are single-use only
  • Wrong O-ring material: Use Viton for most applications, Kalrez for high temp
  • Skipping inspection: A 1-minute check prevents hours of leak hunting

O-Ring Best Practices

✔ DO:

  • Inspect every O-ring before each use
  • Store O-rings away from UV light and ozone
  • Use the correct size for the application
  • Apply thin grease film for easier seating
  • Keep spare O-rings on hand

✘ DON'T:

  • Reuse O-rings that show any damage
  • Stretch O-rings over sharp edges
  • Use excessive vacuum grease
  • Touch O-rings with dirty hands
  • Store O-rings in sunlight
Understanding Vacuum Leaks

A vacuum leak is any unwanted gas entry into the system. Leaks are often the most frustrating problem in vacuum work. Understanding leak types helps with both prevention and diagnosis.

Types of Vacuum Leaks

🔴 Real Leaks

Definition: Actual holes allowing gas flow from atmosphere to vacuum.

Causes: Cracks, poor welds, damaged O-rings, loose fittings

Behavior: Constant leak rate regardless of pump time

Detection: Responds immediately to helium spray

🟠 Virtual Leaks

Definition: Trapped gas volumes that slowly release.

Causes: Blind holes, double O-rings, porous materials, trapped air

Behavior: Leak rate decreases over time

Detection: No response to helium; improves with pump time

🟢 Permeation

Definition: Gas diffusing through solid materials.

Causes: O-rings, gaskets, thin-walled tubing

Behavior: Steady, temperature-dependent rate

Detection: Process of elimination after fixing others

Helium Leak Detection

Helium mass spectrometer leak detection (MSLD) is the gold standard for finding vacuum leaks. A mass spectrometer tuned to mass 4 (helium) detects trace amounts entering the vacuum system.

Why Helium?

🔭 Small Size

Second smallest atom (after H). Penetrates tiny leaks easily.

💨 Inert & Safe

Non-toxic, non-flammable, won't react with anything.

🌡 Low Background

Only ~5 ppm in atmosphere. Low false positive rate.

📈 Easy Detection

Mass 4 is easily separated from other gases by mass spectrometer.

Leak Detection Methods

🔍 Spray Probe Method (Outside-In)

  1. Connect leak detector to vacuum system
  2. Evacuate system to operating pressure
  3. Spray helium at suspected leak points from outside
  4. If helium enters, detector signals leak location

Tips: Start at top (He rises), use small controlled flow, wait for signal response between sprays.

💨 Sniffer Probe Method (Inside-Out)

  1. Pressurize system with helium (or He/air mix)
  2. Move sniffer probe along outside surfaces
  3. Detector measures escaping helium
  4. Higher signal indicates leak location

Best for: Large chambers, systems that can be pressurized, gross leak location.

Hands-On Laboratory: Vacuum System Assembly & Testing

⚠ Safety Precautions — Read Before Starting

  • Eye protection required at all times when working with vacuum systems
  • Handle vacuum pump oil carefully—wipe up spills immediately
  • Never vent a vacuum system rapidly—always use controlled venting
  • Ensure all connections are secure before operating pumps
  • Keep hands and loose clothing away from rotating pump components
  • Report any unusual sounds, smells, or gauge readings to instructor

Part 1: System Assembly
1

Inventory Components

Before assembly, verify all components are present and in good condition. Check O-rings for damage, ensure flanges are clean, and confirm gauge and valve functionality.

2

Prepare Work Area

Clean the work surface with lint-free wipes. Arrange components in assembly order. Put on safety glasses. Ensure adequate lighting.

3

Inspect O-Rings and Centering Rings

Examine each O-ring for cuts, cracks, or embedded particles. Place O-rings in centering rings, ensuring they seat properly in the groove.

4

Assemble KF Connections

Align flanges with centering ring between them. Slide clamp over the junction. Tighten wing nut firmly but do not over-tighten.

5

Connect Gauges and Valves

Attach thermocouple gauge to foreline port. Install vent valve. Connect gate valve between chamber and pump. Verify all connections.

6

Final Inspection

Check each connection. Ensure no O-rings are pinched or displaced. Verify valves are in correct starting positions (usually closed).

Part 2: Pump-Down Procedure
7

Initial Pump-Down (Roughing)

Close all valves. Start the roughing pump. Slowly open the roughing valve. Monitor the thermocouple gauge. Wait until pressure drops below 100 mTorr.

8

Crossover to High Vacuum

Once at crossover pressure (~50 mTorr), close roughing valve. Start turbo pump or open gate valve to diffusion pump. Monitor for normal operation.

9

Achieve Base Pressure

Open gate valve to chamber. Monitor ion gauge as pressure drops. Record time to reach target pressures.

10

System Shutdown

Close gate valve. Allow turbo pump to spin down completely. Slowly vent system using vent valve—never rapid venting! Turn off roughing pump after venting.

Discussion Questions
Turbomolecular pumps can only operate efficiently at low pressures (below ~0.1 Torr). They cannot exhaust directly to atmosphere because they rely on molecular flow regime. The backing pump maintains the low exhaust pressure needed for the turbo to function.
Catastrophic failure. Air rushing into the spinning rotor (20,000–90,000 RPM) creates massive aerodynamic forces that can shatter the blades, destroy the bearings, and potentially eject metal fragments. Always wait for complete spin-down before venting.
Systematic troubleshooting: (1) Check all O-rings and fittings for damage; (2) Perform rate-of-rise test to distinguish real leak from outgassing; (3) Check pump oil level and condition; (4) Verify all valves are in correct positions; (5) Use helium leak detector if available; (6) Consider longer pump time for water vapor outgassing.
Water vapor from the atmosphere adsorbs onto all internal surfaces. This water slowly desorbs (outgasses) during pumping, limiting the achievable pressure. The effect is worse at higher humidity and with longer exposure times. This is why UHV systems require bakeout.
