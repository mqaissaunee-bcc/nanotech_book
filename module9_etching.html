Section 9.1 — What Is Etching?

In semiconductor fabrication, etching is the controlled removal of material from a substrate to transfer a pattern defined by a mask. If photolithography is the “photography” of the semiconductor world, etching is the “sculpture” — it carves the pattern into the actual device layers.

Every integrated circuit, MEMS device, and nanoscale sensor requires dozens to hundreds of etching steps during fabrication. A modern microprocessor may undergo more than 60 individual etch steps to define transistor gates, interconnect trenches, contact holes, and isolation structures. The challenge at each step: remove exactly the right material, in exactly the right places, to exactly the right depth — without damaging anything else.

💡 The Big Picture

The fabrication sequence is cyclical: Deposit → Pattern (Lithography) → Etch → Repeat. This cycle may be performed 30–60+ times to build a modern chip. Etching is the subtractive step that defines device geometry.

Etching in the Fabrication Sequence

Etching occurs immediately after the photolithography step. A thin film has been deposited, a photoresist pattern defined on top, and now the exposed regions of the thin film must be removed. The photoresist acts as a protective etch mask, shielding the areas underneath while the etchant attacks the unprotected film.

The Two Fundamental Classes of Etching

💧 Wet Etching

The substrate is immersed in a liquid chemical solution that dissolves the target material through chemical reaction. This is the oldest and simplest etching technique.

Key traits: Generally isotropic, high selectivity, excellent throughput via batch processing, strong temperature dependence, lower equipment cost.

⚡ Dry Etching

Reactive gases in a plasma state attack the substrate through a combination of chemical reactions and physical ion bombardment.

Key traits: Can be highly anisotropic, moderate selectivity, excellent reproducibility, single-wafer processing, higher equipment cost.

Section 9.2 — Isotropic vs. Anisotropic Etch Profiles

The most critical distinction in etching is the directionality of material removal. This determines the shape of the etch profile and ultimately what feature sizes are achievable.

⚫ Isotropic Etching

“Iso” = same, “tropic” = direction. The etch proceeds at the same rate in all directions. This produces a rounded cavity with undercut beneath the mask approximately equal to the etch depth.

Typical method: Wet chemical etching

Best for: Bulk removal, cleaning, MEMS cavities where undercut is tolerable

⚫ Anisotropic Etching

“An” = not. The etch rate varies with direction. In plasma dry etching, ions bombard vertically, so the vertical rate far exceeds lateral. This produces steep, near-vertical sidewalls with minimal undercut.

Typical method: RIE, ICP-RIE, DRIE

Best for: Critical dimension patterning, high-aspect-ratio trenches, sub-micron IC features

Degree of Anisotropy (A)

A = 1 − (Rlateral / Rvertical)

Where Rlateral is the lateral etch rate and Rvertical is the vertical etch rate.

  • A = 0 → Perfectly isotropic (equal in all directions)
  • A = 1 → Perfectly anisotropic (zero lateral etching)
  • A = 0.5–0.9 → Typical range for RIE processes

🧡 Crystal-Dependent Anisotropic Wet Etching

A special exception: KOH etching of silicon is an anisotropic wet etch. KOH etches the Si(100) crystal plane approximately 400× faster than the Si(111) plane, producing characteristic V-grooves with a precise 54.7° angle. This is widely used in MEMS to create diaphragms, channels, and through-wafer vias.

Section 9.3 — Wet Etching: Principles and Chemistries

Wet etching is conceptually simple: immerse a substrate in a chemical solution that dissolves the target material. Despite its simplicity, wet etching remains essential — roughly 30–40% of all etch steps in semiconductor fabrication are still wet processes.

Mechanism of Wet Etching

The wet etch process involves three sequential steps:

  1. Diffusion: Etchant molecules diffuse through solution to reach the surface
  2. Reaction: Chemical reaction forms soluble byproducts
  3. Removal: Reaction products dissolve and diffuse away into bulk solution

If the reaction is rate-limiting, the process is reaction-rate limited (strongly temperature-dependent). If diffusion is limiting, it is transport limited (dependent on agitation).

⚠️ Critical Safety: Hydrofluoric Acid (HF)

HF is the standard etchant for SiO₂ but is extremely dangerous. Unlike most acids that cause immediate pain, HF penetrates skin painlessly and attacks underlying bone by binding calcium. Even small exposures can be fatal. In fabs, HF handling requires specialized training, calcium gluconate antidote gel on-hand, buddy systems, and specific PPE. HF should never be used in educational settings without proper institutional safety protocols.

Section 9.4 — Dry Etching: From Sputtering to RIE

Dry etching uses reactive gases in a plasma state. Its evolution tracks the industry’s march toward smaller features — each new technology node demanded more precise directional control than wet etching could provide.

The Spectrum of Dry Etching

Physical: Ion Milling

Inert gas ions (Ar⁺) sputter material by momentum transfer — pure physical bombardment.

Anisotropy: Very high • Selectivity: Very low • Damage: High

Chemical: Plasma Etching

Reactive radicals (F*, Cl*, O*) diffuse to the substrate and react chemically. No directed ion bombardment.

Anisotropy: Very low • Selectivity: Very high • Damage: Very low

💡 The Best of Both Worlds: Reactive Ion Etching (RIE)

RIE combines chemical reactivity with physical ion bombardment. Reactive gas ions are accelerated vertically onto the substrate by the DC self-bias. Ions break bonds on horizontal surfaces while sidewalls see minimal bombardment. Result: high anisotropy AND reasonable selectivity.

How RIE Works

  1. Reactive gas fills the chamber at low pressure (10–100 mTorr)
  2. RF field (13.56 MHz) strips electrons, creating plasma with ions, electrons, and radicals
  3. DC self-bias develops on cathode, accelerating positive ions vertically toward substrate
  4. Ions bombard horizontal surfaces, breaking bonds and enabling chemical reactions
  5. Volatile reaction products (e.g., SiF₄) are pumped away

ICP-RIE: Higher Density, Better Control

ICP-RIE is the workhorse of modern etching. It uses two independent RF sources: one to generate high-density plasma via inductive coil, and one to control ion energy at the substrate. This decoupling enables high etch rates while minimizing substrate damage.

Section 9.5 — Selectivity, Etch Rate, and Process Control

Etch Rate (R)

R = d / t

Where d = thickness removed and t = etch time. Expressed in nm/min or µm/min.

Selectivity (S)

S = Rtarget / Rmask

Ratio of etch rate of target material to mask (or stop layer). Higher selectivity = better mask preservation.

  • Wet etching: Selectivity can exceed 100:1 to 1000:1
  • Dry etching (RIE): Typical 3:1 to 30:1
  • Ion milling: Near 1:1 (poor)

📈 Worked Example

A 500 nm SiO₂ film is etched in BOE for 5 min (fully removed). Etch rate = 500 nm / 5 min = 100 nm/min.

If the photoresist lost 25 nm during the same etch: Selectivity = 100 / (25/5) = 20:1.

Section 9.6 — DRIE and the Bosch Process

DRIE can create trenches hundreds of micrometers deep with aspect ratios exceeding 50:1 — the backbone of MEMS fabrication and 3D chip packaging.

The Bosch Process: Alternating Etch and Passivation

The Bosch process achieves deep anisotropic etching by rapidly alternating two steps:

Step 1: Etch (SF₆)

SF₆ plasma generates fluorine radicals that isotropically etch silicon for 3–12 seconds, removing a small amount in all directions.

Step 2: Passivate (C₄F₈)

C₄F₈ plasma deposits a thin Teflon-like fluorocarbon polymer on all surfaces — protecting sidewalls from the next etch step.

When etching resumes, directional ions remove passivation from the horizontal trench bottom while vertical sidewalls retain protection. This cycle repeats hundreds to thousands of times, progressively deepening the trench.

🔎 Sidewall Scalloping

Each etch-passivate cycle creates a tiny lateral divot, producing characteristic scalloped sidewalls with 50–200 nm roughness. For applications requiring smooth walls, cycle times are shortened or post-etch smoothing is applied.

Loading Effect

Etch rate depends on total exposed silicon area. More exposed area consumes more radicals, reducing rate.

Microloading (ARDE)

Narrower trenches etch slower than wider ones because fewer radicals diffuse to the bottom. A 5 µm trench may etch 30% slower than a 50 µm trench.

🏭 Industry Applications of DRIE

MEMS accelerometers and gyroscopes (every smartphone), through-silicon vias (TSVs) for 3D chip stacking, microfluidic channels for lab-on-a-chip devices, silicon photonics waveguides, and micro-mirror arrays for DLP projectors. The global MEMS market exceeds $15 billion annually.

Section 9.7 — Atomic Layer Etching: The Frontier

As transistors shrink below 5 nm, even RIE becomes too coarse. Atomic Layer Etching (ALE) removes material one atomic layer at a time with self-limiting precision.

How ALE Works

  1. Surface Modification: Reactive gas modifies exactly one atomic layer (e.g., Cl₂ chemisorbs on Si forming SiClx)
  2. Removal: Low-energy ions or a second species remove only the modified layer. Self-limiting — stops automatically after one layer.

Each cycle removes ~0.05–0.3 nm. The self-limiting nature prevents over-etching — critical for atomic-scale devices.

🚀 ALE Market and Future

The ALE equipment market was valued at approximately $740 million in 2024 and is projected to exceed $1.3 billion by 2032. ALE is considered essential for gate-all-around (GAA) transistors, 3D NAND with 200+ layers, and advanced packaging. Major manufacturers including Lam Research, Applied Materials, and Tokyo Electron are investing heavily in ALE development.

💡 Lab Connection

In our lab, we use a Sharpie as an etch mask — an organic polymer applied by hand. In industry, the mask is photoresist patterned by UV lithography (Module 8). Both serve the same purpose; the Sharpie just has much lower resolution (~500 µm vs. sub-100 nm).

Etch Kinetics: Temperature and the Arrhenius Relationship

Arrhenius Equation for Etch Rate

R = A · e−Ea/(kBT)

Where R = etch rate, A = pre-exponential factor, Ea = activation energy (eV), kB = Boltzmann constant (8.617 × 10−5 eV/K), T = temperature (K).

Rule of thumb: for every 10°C increase, the etch rate roughly doubles. A 2°C fluctuation can cause 10–15% variation in etch rate, which is why wet etch baths require precise temperature control.

Plasma Physics in RIE

Ions (e.g., CF₃⁺, Ar⁺)

Accelerated vertically by DC self-bias. Kinetic energy of 50–500 eV provides the physical bombardment component, creating anisotropic profiles.

Radicals (e.g., F*, Cl*, O*)

Neutral species with unpaired electrons. Not affected by electric fields, so they move randomly (isotropically), providing the chemistry-selective etching component.

📊 The Coburn-Winters Experiment (1979)

The landmark experiment: XeF₂ alone etched Si at ~1 nm/min, Ar⁺ beam alone at ~3 nm/min, but XeF₂ + Ar⁺ simultaneously gave ~15 nm/min — 5× greater than the sum. This proved ion bombardment synergistically enhances chemical etching rather than simply adding to it.

Endpoint Detection

Optical Emission Spectroscopy (OES)

Monitors light emitted by plasma species. When the target film is consumed, reaction product concentrations change, altering the emission spectrum to signal endpoint.

Laser Interferometry

A laser reflects off the film; as it thins, interference creates oscillating reflectivity. Oscillation period reveals etch rate; oscillations stopping indicates film removal.

Hands-On Lab: Wet Etching of Aluminum with Ferric Chloride

Perform wet etching of aluminum using FeCl₃ solution, create a patterned mask using a Sharpie marker, measure the etch rate, and compare results to published values.

⚠️ Safety Requirements

  • Wear safety goggles at all times
  • Wear nitrile gloves when handling FeCl₃
  • Work in a well-ventilated area or under a fume hood
  • FeCl₃ permanently stains — wear a lab coat
  • Do not pour FeCl₃ down the drain — dispose per instructor guidelines
  • Rinse immediately with water if FeCl₃ contacts skin

Materials & Equipment

Materials

  • Aluminum disk substrates
  • Ferric chloride solution (FeCl₃)
  • Fine-tip Sharpie permanent marker
  • Acetone and DI water
  • Paper towels / cleanroom wipes

Equipment

  • Plastic container for etchant
  • Thermometer
  • Timer / stopwatch
  • Digital calipers (optional)
  • Tweezers (plastic-tipped)

Procedure

1

Prepare the Substrate

Clean the aluminum disk with acetone, then DI water. Dry with lint-free wipe. Surface should be uniformly shiny with no fingerprints or debris.

2

Apply the Etch Mask

Draw your design on the aluminum using the Sharpie. The ink acts as the etch mask — protected areas stay shiny. Apply in a single, even coat and allow 2–3 minutes to dry.

Industry connection: This is analogous to photoresist from Module 8. The Sharpie is a low-cost stand-in.

3

Prepare the Etchant Bath

Pour FeCl₃ solution to ~1–2 cm depth. Record temperature. If heating, warm to 40–50°C. Do not exceed 60°C.

Chemical reaction: 2 FeCl₃ + Al → AlCl₃ + 2 FeCl₂

4

Immerse and Etch

Place substrate face-down in etchant (gravity helps byproducts fall away). Start timer. Typical time: 30–60 min at room temperature; 10–20 min at 50°C.

5

Monitor Progress

Check every 5–10 min. Exposed areas become darker and rougher. Etch is complete when exposed areas are clearly differentiated from protected areas.

6

Rinse and Remove Mask

Rinse thoroughly with DI water to stop the reaction. Soak/spray with acetone to dissolve the Sharpie mask. Rinse again and dry.

7

Inspect Results

Etched areas appear darker/pitted; protected areas are shiny/smooth. If measurement tools are available, measure step height to calculate etch rate.
