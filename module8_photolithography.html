Part 1: The Heart of Semiconductor Fabrication

Why Photolithography Matters

PhotolithographyA microfabrication process that uses light to transfer geometric patterns from a photomask to a light-sensitive chemical (photoresist) on a substrate. Also called optical lithography or UV lithography. Approximately one-third of all semiconductor processing steps involve photolithography. is the single most critical process in semiconductor manufacturing. Approximately one-third of all CMOS transistor processing steps relate directly to photolithography. Every transistor, interconnect, and structure on a modern microchip is defined through repeated cycles of this patterning technique.

A state-of-the-art EUV lithographyExtreme Ultraviolet Lithography uses 13.5 nm wavelength light from a laser-pulsed tin plasma to pattern features below 7 nm. ASML is the sole manufacturer of EUV systems, which cost over $300 million each. The technology requires reflective optics and specialized photoresists. scanner from ASML costs over $300 million per unit, making it among the most expensive pieces of equipment in any industry. This massive investment reflects the extraordinary precision required to print billions of transistors on a chip smaller than your fingernail.

🏭 Industry Scale

Modern processors like Apple's M4 contain over 28 billion transistors. Each transistor is defined through photolithography steps that must align within a few nanometers — less than the diameter of a strand of DNA. The global lithography equipment market exceeds $20 billion annually, with ASML holding 100% of the EUV market. In December 2025, Intel deployed the world's first production-ready High-NA EUV system — the ASML TWINSCAN EXE:5200B costing approximately $400 million — achieving 8 nm resolution for its 14A process node.

The Stencil Analogy

To understand photolithography, imagine painting road markings:

  1. Place a stencil (photomask) on the road (substrate)
  2. Spray paint (UV light) over the stencil
  3. Remove the stencil to reveal the pattern underneath

In photolithography, the key components map as follows:

🎨 Analogy

Road = Substrate (silicon wafer)

Stencil = Photomask

Paint = Photoresist

Spray action = UV light exposure

🔎 Key Difference

Unlike road painting, photolithography works chemically. The UV light changes the photoresist's molecular structure, making it either soluble or insoluble in a developer solution. This chemical change enables incredibly precise pattern transfer at the nanometer scale.

💡 Why HMDS Matters

HMDSHexamethyldisilazane, chemical formula (CH₃)₃SiNHSi(CH₃)₃. A primer applied to silicon wafers before photoresist coating. It reacts with surface hydroxyl groups to form a hydrophobic monolayer, dramatically improving resist adhesion. Without HMDS, resist can peel during development, causing pattern defects. (hexamethyldisilazane) is like a paint primer on a wall. A clean silicon wafer surface is naturally hydrophilic — it attracts water. Photoresist is hydrophobic — it repels water. Without HMDS, the resist will not adhere properly, leading to delamination and pattern defects during development. HMDS creates a molecular bridge between the two incompatible surfaces.

Part 3: Photoresist Chemistry

What Is Photoresist?

A photoresistA light-sensitive polymeric material used in photolithography to form a patterned coating on a surface. Composed of three main components: a base polymer (resin) that provides structural properties, a photoactive compound (PAC) that responds to UV light, and a solvent that controls viscosity for spin coating. Industry examples include Shipley S1813, AZ 5214, and SU-8. is a light-sensitive polymer that undergoes chemical changes when exposed to specific wavelengths of ultraviolet light. It consists of three main components:

🧪 Base Polymer (Resin)

Provides the structural backbone and mechanical properties. Determines thermal stability and etch resistance. Common polymers include novolak (for positive resists) and epoxy-based SU-8 (for negative resists).

☀️ Photoactive Compound (PAC)

The light-sensitive ingredient that changes the resist's solubility upon UV exposure. In positive resists, the PAC (diazonaphthoquinone, DNQ) converts from dissolution inhibitor to dissolution promoter. In chemically amplified resists, photoacid generators (PAGs) produce acid catalysts.

💧 Solvent

Controls viscosity for spin coating. Common solvents include PGMEA (propylene glycol monomethyl ether acetate). The solvent evaporates during the soft bake step, leaving a solid film. Solvent content determines spin-curve behavior.

Positive vs. Negative Photoresist

The two types of photoresist produce opposite results when exposed to UV light:

🔵 Positive Photoresist

Behavior: UV-exposed areas become soluble and wash away in developer.

Chemistry: DNQ photoactive compound absorbs UV light and converts to indenecarboxylic acid, which is soluble in aqueous base developer (TMAH).

Resolution: Higher resolution (finer features)

Use: >90% of IC manufacturing

Example: Shipley S1813, AZ 1500 series

💬 “What Shows, Goes”

🔴 Negative Photoresist

Behavior: UV-exposed areas become insoluble (cross-linked) and remain after development.

Chemistry: UV light initiates cross-linking reactions between polymer chains, making exposed areas resistant to solvents and developers.

Resolution: Lower resolution but better adhesion & chemical resistance

Use: Thick films, MEMS, microfluidics

Example: SU-8, UV gel nail polish

💬 “What Shows, Stays”

🎓 Lab Connection

In our hands-on lab, we use UV-curable gel nail polish as a negative photoresist analog. When exposed to UV light, the gel cross-links and hardens — the exposed areas stay on the substrate. The unexposed areas are washed away with IPA and acetone during development. This is the same fundamental principle used in SU-8 photoresist processing in industry.

Part 4: Spin Coating — Creating Uniform Thin Films

The Physics of Spin Coating

Spin coatingA technique to create uniform thin films by depositing liquid material on a spinning substrate. Centrifugal force spreads the liquid outward while viscous forces and solvent evaporation determine the final thickness. The process has four stages: deposition, spin-up, spin-off, and evaporation. Film thickness varies inversely with the square root of spin speed. uses centrifugal force to spread a liquid uniformly across a spinning substrate. The technique follows four distinct stages:

Stage 1: Deposition

Liquid photoresist is dispensed onto the center of the substrate (static or dynamic dispense). Excess material is applied to ensure complete coverage.

Stage 2: Spin-Up

The substrate accelerates to the target spin speed. Centrifugal force drives excess resist outward. Fast acceleration ensures uniform initial spreading.

Stage 3: Spin-Off

At constant speed, the film thins gradually as excess resist flies off the edges. The balance between centrifugal force and viscous resistance governs thinning rate.

Stage 4: Evaporation

Solvent evaporates, increasing viscosity and “setting” the film. This is the dominant mechanism determining final thickness. The film gels when flow stops.

Empirical Spin Coating Relationship

t ∝ 1 / √ω

Film thickness (t) varies inversely with the square root of the angular velocity (ω) or spin speed in RPM. Doubling the spin speed reduces thickness by approximately 30%, not 50%. This is the fundamental engineering relationship used to select spin parameters for a desired thickness.

A more complete semi-empirical model (Meyerhofer, 1978) accounts for viscosity and evaporation rate:

tf = k · c0γ · η1/3 · ω-1/2

Where k = constant, c0 = initial concentration, γ ≈ 2.5 for photoresists, η = viscosity, ω = angular velocity. In practice, engineers use spin curves — empirically measured thickness vs. RPM plots supplied by photoresist manufacturers.

Part 5: Photomasks & UV Exposure

Photomask Design and Function

A photomaskA quartz or glass plate with a precisely patterned chrome (or other absorber) layer that selectively blocks UV light. In production, masks cost $100,000–$500,000+ each and require weeks to fabricate. A single advanced chip design may require 80–100 mask layers. Defects as small as a few nanometers can render a mask unusable. is a precisely fabricated template that contains the circuit patterns to be transferred to the wafer. It works like a sophisticated stencil — opaque areas block UV light while transparent areas allow it through.

Mask Components

Substrate: Ultra-pure fused silica (quartz) with near-zero thermal expansion

Absorber: Thin chrome layer (~100 nm) that blocks UV light

Pellicle: Protective transparent membrane that keeps particles out of the focal plane

Size: Typically 6″ × 6″ (152 mm) for 4× reduction stepper systems

Mask Types

Binary masks: Simple opaque/transparent patterns (most common)

Phase-shift masks (PSM): Use phase differences in transmitted light to enhance resolution

Attenuated PSM: Partially transparent absorber shifts phase by 180°

EUV masks: Reflective (not transmissive) due to EUV absorption by all materials

Exposure Systems

There are three main exposure configurations, each with different capabilities:

Contact/Proximity Printing

Method: Mask directly contacts (or nearly contacts) the resist-coated wafer

Resolution: ~1–2 µm

Advantage: Simple, low cost

Disadvantage: Mask damage, defects from contact

Used in: Research labs, our lab experiment

Projection (Stepper)

Method: Lens system projects mask image at 4× or 5× reduction

Resolution: 0.25–1 µm

Advantage: No mask contact, high resolution

Disadvantage: Complex optics, field-by-field exposure

Used in: Production fabs (DUV)

Scanner

Method: Slit illumination scans across mask & wafer simultaneously

Resolution: <10 nm (with EUV)

Advantage: Highest resolution, large field size

Disadvantage: >$300M cost (EUV)

Used in: Leading-edge fabs (TSMC, Samsung, Intel)

Part 6: Resolution Limits & the Rayleigh Criterion

What Limits How Small We Can Print?

The fundamental resolution of any optical lithography system is governed by the Rayleigh criterionA formula from classical optics that defines the minimum feature size (resolution) achievable by an imaging system. For photolithography: R = k₁ × λ / NA, where R is the minimum feature size, k₁ is a process-dependent constant (typically 0.25–0.8), λ is the exposure wavelength, and NA is the numerical aperture of the projection lens., which relates minimum feature size to the wavelength of light and the lens optics:

Rayleigh Resolution Equation

R = k1 × λ / NA

R = minimum resolvable feature size (half-pitch)

k1 = process factor (0.25 ≤ k1 ≤ 0.8; lower = more aggressive)

λ = exposure wavelength

NA = numerical aperture of the projection lens

To print smaller features, engineers have three “knobs” to turn:

1. Reduce λ (Wavelength)

The most impactful approach. Moving from 436 nm (g-line) to 13.5 nm (EUV) allows features 30× smaller. Each wavelength generation required entirely new light sources, optics, and resist materials.

2. Increase NA (Numerical Aperture)

Larger lenses capture more diffracted light. Immersion lithography (water between lens and wafer) increased NA from ~0.93 to 1.35. High-NA EUV pushes NA from 0.33 to 0.55.

3. Reduce k1 (Process Factor)

Computational tricks: off-axis illumination, phase-shift masks, optical proximity correction (OPC), and multi-patterning. Modern fabs operate near the theoretical limit of k1 = 0.25.

Rayleigh Depth of Focus

DOF = k2 × λ / NA2

The depth of focus (DOF)The range of vertical distance over which the projected image remains acceptably sharp. A larger DOF is desirable because it allows for surface flatness variations and resist thickness non-uniformity. Unfortunately, increasing NA to improve resolution reduces DOF, creating a fundamental tradeoff. defines the vertical range over which the projected image stays sharp. Notice that DOF decreases with the square of NA, creating a fundamental tradeoff: higher resolution means less tolerance for wafer flatness variation. This is why wafer surface planarity (via CMP) is critical in advanced nodes.

EUV Lithography: The Current Frontier

Extreme Ultraviolet lithography represents a revolutionary leap. At 13.5 nm, EUV light is actually soft X-ray radiation that is absorbed by virtually all materials, including air and glass. This creates unique engineering challenges:

🔴 EUV Challenges

No lenses: All optics must be reflective (multilayer Mo/Si mirrors), not refractive

Vacuum required: EUV is absorbed by air; entire optical path must be in vacuum

Low source power: Only ~1% of generated light reaches the wafer after 12+ mirror reflections

Mask defects: Reflective masks require defect-free multilayer coatings

Stochastic effects: Fewer photons per feature increases shot noise and random errors

🟢 EUV Breakthroughs

Tin plasma source: 50 kHz pulsed CO2 laser vaporizes tin droplets to produce EUV

Throughput: ASML NXE:3800E achieves >185 wafers/hour

High-NA EUV: TWINSCAN EXE:5200B increases NA from 0.33 to 0.55. Prints 1.7x smaller features with 2.9x higher transistor density. 175 wafers/hour throughput with 0.7 nm overlay accuracy.

Single exposure: Eliminates costly multi-patterning for critical layers

AI integration: Machine learning optimizes exposure and defect detection in real time

💰 The Economics of EUV

An ASML EUV scanner costs over $300 million. The High-NA version (EXE:5200B) costs approximately $400 million. A single advanced fab may contain 15–20 EUV systems. ASML currently holds 100% market share for EUV lithography. The company invested over €6 billion and 17 years of R&D to bring EUV to production. In December 2025, Intel installed the first production-ready High-NA EUV system (TWINSCAN EXE:5200B), capable of 8 nm resolution and processing 175 wafers per hour with 0.7 nm overlay accuracy. Samsung and SK hynix also received High-NA systems in late 2025 for memory applications. High-volume manufacturing with High-NA is expected in 2027–2028, and ASML is already developing “Hyper-NA” at 0.75 NA for insertion after 2030.

Part 8: Anti-Reflective Coatings & Advanced Techniques

Why Reflections Are the Enemy

When UV light passes through photoresist and hits a reflective substrate (like aluminum or silicon), some light bounces back up through the resist, creating standing wavesConstructive and destructive interference patterns formed when incident and reflected UV light waves interact within the photoresist layer. Standing waves create periodic thickness variations in the developed resist profile (scalloped sidewalls), degrading pattern fidelity. The post-exposure bake (PEB) helps reduce standing wave effects through thermal diffusion of the photoactive compound. — alternating bright and dark fringes within the resist film. These standing waves cause scalloped resist sidewalls and non-uniform exposure.

BARC (Bottom Anti-Reflective Coating)

Applied under the photoresist, directly on the substrate. Absorbs or destructively interferes with reflected light before it can re-enter the resist.

Materials: Spin-on organic polymers or inorganic films (SiON)

Benefit: Eliminates substrate reflections, reduces CD (critical dimension) variation

TARC (Top Anti-Reflective Coating)

Applied on top of the photoresist. Reduces reflections from the resist surface itself, particularly important for highly reflective substrates.

Materials: Water-soluble polymers (removed during development)

Benefit: Improves dose uniformity across the wafer, reduces swing curve effects

🎓 Lab Connection: The Black Tape Trick

In our low-cost lab experiment, we place a strip of black tape underneath the glass slide during UV exposure. This serves exactly the same purpose as an industrial BARC — it absorbs UV light that would otherwise reflect off the back surface of the glass slide. Without this modification, back reflections cause diffuse exposure and poor pattern definition.

Additional Advanced Techniques

Immersion Lithography

Water (n = 1.44) fills the gap between the final lens element and the wafer, increasing NA beyond 1.0. This extended 193 nm lithography to 22 nm nodes, delaying the need for EUV by nearly a decade.

Multi-Patterning

Using two or more exposures to create features smaller than a single exposure can resolve. Techniques include LELE (litho-etch-litho-etch) and SADP (self-aligned double patterning). Increases cost and process complexity significantly.

Computational Lithography

Optical proximity correction (OPC) modifies mask patterns to compensate for diffraction effects. Sub-resolution assist features (SRAFs) improve pattern fidelity. Machine learning now optimizes mask designs automatically.

🛑 SAFETY FIRST: Required Precautions

This experiment involves UV light exposure, chemical solvents, and a high-speed spinning platform. All safety protocols must be followed at all times.

  • Eye Protection: Wear UV-blocking safety glasses during exposure. NEVER look directly at the UV light source.
  • Skin Protection: Wear nitrile gloves when handling chemicals and photoresist
  • Ventilation: Work in a fume hood or well-ventilated area when using acetone and IPA
  • Clothing: Wear lab coat and closed-toe shoes; keep loose clothing away from spinner
  • Chemical Handling: Know the location of eyewash station and spill kit before beginning
  • Hot Surfaces: Use caution with the hotplate; handle samples with tweezers only
Experiment Overview: Low-Cost Photolithography

In this experiment, you will perform a simplified photolithography process using UV-curable gel nail polish as a negative photoresist analog. Step-by-Step Procedure
1

Substrate Cleaning

Clean the glass slide using the standard solvent cleaning sequence:

  • Rinse thoroughly with acetone to remove organic contaminants
  • Rinse with IPA to remove the acetone
  • Dry with a lint-free wipe or blow dry with compressed air
  • Handle only by edges to avoid fingerprints
2

Photomask Preparation

Create your photomask using transparency film:

  • Draw or print a pattern on the transparency film using permanent marker or laser printer
  • The dark (opaque) areas will block UV light
  • The clear (transparent) areas will transmit UV light
  • Remember: we are using negative resist — exposed areas will remain
3

Resist Application (Spin Coating)

Apply a thin, uniform layer of gel nail polish:

  • Apply 2–3 drops of gel nail polish to the center of the clean slide
  • Spin the sample at moderate speed for 30 seconds using the spin coater (or laptop fan)
  • The goal is a thin, even coating — thinner coats produce better resolution
  • Note: Higher spin speed = thinner film (inversely proportional to √RPM)
4

Pre-Exposure Bake (Soft Bake)

Stabilize the resist film before exposure:

  • Place the coated slide on the hotplate at 100°C
  • Bake for 2 minutes to drive off volatile solvents
  • Remove carefully using tweezers and allow to cool

⚠️ Hot Surface Warning

The hotplate and sample will be very hot. Use tweezers to handle the slide. Never touch the hotplate surface directly.

5

Anti-Reflective Modification

Apply the low-cost BARC analog:

  • Place a strip of black electrical tape on the bottom surface of the glass slide
  • This absorbs UV light that would otherwise reflect off the back surface
  • Without this step, back reflections cause diffuse exposure and poor pattern fidelity
6

UV Exposure

Transfer the mask pattern into the photoresist:

  • Place the photomask pattern-side DOWN directly on the coated slide
  • Ensure intimate contact between mask and resist (no air gap)
  • Place under the UV nail lamp
  • Expose for exactly 5 seconds
  • Remove immediately after exposure

🛑 UV Safety

DO NOT look directly at the UV light source! UV radiation causes severe eye damage. Wear UV-blocking safety glasses throughout this step.

7

Post-Exposure Bake

Stabilize the exposed pattern:

  • Separate the mask from the exposed slide carefully
  • Place on the hotplate at 155°C
  • Bake for 5 minutes
  • Remove and allow to cool completely
8

Development

Reveal the pattern by removing the unexposed photoresist:

  • Submerge the slide in an IPA bath and agitate gently for 2 minutes
  • Spray with acetone (may need to let it sit briefly)
  • Give a second, more forceful spray if residue remains
  • Rinse with IPA
  • Pat dry with a lint-free wipe or blow dry

💡 Remember: Negative Resist

This process uses negative lithography — the UV-exposed areas become hardened (cross-linked) and remain on the slide. The unexposed areas are washed away during development. This is “What Shows, Stays.”
