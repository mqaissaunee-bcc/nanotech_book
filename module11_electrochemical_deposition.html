⚡ Part 1: Introduction to Electrochemical Deposition

Electrochemical depositionA process using electrical current to reduce dissolved metal ions onto a conductive surface (cathode), building thin films atom by atom with precise thickness control. (also called electroplating or electrodeposition) operates at atmospheric pressure and near room temperature, making it fundamentally different from the vacuum-based PVD and CVD methods studied previously. Luigi Brugnatelli first electrodeposited gold in 1805 using a voltaic pile. Today, electrodeposition creates the copper interconnectsMiles of nanoscale copper wiring inside modern microprocessors connecting billions of transistors. Copper replaced aluminum starting in 1997 due to its 40% lower resistance. inside every modern microprocessor.

Why Electrodeposition Matters

Electrodeposition occupies a unique niche among thin film techniques: precise thickness control via Faraday’s laws, no vacuum equipment needed, selective deposition on conductors only, ability to fill high-aspect-ratio trenches and vias, and scalability from benchtop demonstrations to full-wafer manufacturing. In 1997, IBM shipped the first processor with electroplated copper wiring, launching the “copper revolution.”

🏭 Industry Scale

A modern processor contains over 10 copper metal layers, with the finest wires just 20–30 nm wide. Total copper wiring per chip can exceed 100 kilometers—all deposited via electroplating using the same fundamental chemistry explored in this module.

Every electrochemical process is built upon redox reactionsReactions involving electron transfer between species. One species loses electrons (oxidation) while another gains them (reduction). These paired half-reactions always occur together.—reactions in which electrons transfer between species.

🔋 Part 3: Electrochemical Cells

Two fundamental cell types, distinguished by whether the reaction is spontaneous or driven externally:

🔋 Galvanic (Voltaic) Cell

  • Reaction: Spontaneous
  • Energy: Chemical → Electrical
  • Purpose: Generate electricity
  • Examples: Batteries, fuel cells
  • ΔG: Negative
  • E°cell: Positive

⚡ Electrolytic Cell

  • Reaction: Non-spontaneous
  • Energy: Electrical → Chemical
  • Purpose: Drive desired reactions
  • Examples: Electroplating, electrolysis
  • ΔG: Positive
  • E°cell: Negative (needs external V)

Electrodeposition uses an electrolytic cell: external power forces a non-spontaneous reduction to deposit metal atoms onto the cathode.

🔑 Electrode Identification

In an electrolytic cell: cathode connects to the negative terminal (attracting positive ions); anode connects to positive. Remember “Red Cat” (reduction at cathode) and “An Ox” (oxidation at anode).

📜 Part 4: Faraday’s Laws of Electrolysis

Michael Faraday’s 1830s experiments established the quantitative link between electrical charge and chemical change, enabling precise prediction of deposited mass.

Faraday’s First Law

Mass deposited is directly proportional to total electric charge:

m = Z × Q = Z × I × t
m = mass (g), Z = electrochemical equivalent (g/C), Q = charge (C), I = current (A), t = time (s)

Faraday’s Second Law

Masses deposited from different electrolytes by the same charge are proportional to their equivalent weightsMolar mass divided by number of electrons. For Cu²⁺ (M=63.55, n=2): 31.78 g/eq. For Au³⁺ (M=196.97, n=3): 65.66 g/eq.:

m = (I × t × M) / (n × F)
M = molar mass (g/mol), n = electrons transferred, F = 96,485 C/mol

The Faraday Constant

F = NA × e = 96,485 C/mol
NA = 6.022 × 10²³ (Avogadro), e = 1.602 × 10−¹&sup9; C (elementary charge)

Current Efficiency

Side reactions (hydrogen evolution, etc.) consume some current, so actual yield is always less than theoretical:

η = (mactual / mtheoretical) × 100%
Typical: Gold ~90–99%, Copper ~95–99%, Chromium ~10–25%

📝 Worked Example

Problem: Mass of gold deposited at 5.0 mA for 10 min, 95% efficiency?

m = (0.005 × 600 × 196.97) / (3 × 96485) = 590.91 / 289455 = 2.04 mg (theoretical)

Actual: 2.04 × 0.95 = 1.94 mg

📊 Part 5: The Nernst Equation

Standard potentials apply only at 1 M, 298 K. In real baths, concentrations vary. The Nernst equationRelates electrode potential to ion concentration under non-standard conditions. Developed by Walther Nernst (1889), connecting thermodynamics to electrochemistry. gives the actual potential:

E = E° − (RT / nF) × ln Q
General Nernst equation

At 25°C (298 K):

E = E° − (0.0592 / n) × log Q
E = actual potential (V), n = electrons, Q = reaction quotient

Practical Implications

As deposition proceeds, ion concentration near the cathode drops, shifting the potential. Bath agitation (stirring) replenishes ions and maintains uniform deposition. The Nernst equation also connects to thermodynamics:

ΔG = −nFEcell
ΔG < 0: spontaneous (galvanic) | ΔG > 0: non-spontaneous (electrolytic)
🌱 Part 6: Film Growth & Optical Properties

Electrodeposited films grow through stages similar to PVD but in a liquid environment:

1. Ion Transport

Cations migrate to cathode via diffusion, convection, and electromigration.

2. Charge Transfer

Ions accept electrons at the surface and reduce to metal adatoms.

3. Nucleation

Adatoms cluster at favorable sites (defects, grain boundaries) to form nuclei.

4. Coalescence

Nuclei grow into islands that merge into a continuous polycrystalline film.

🏭 Part 7: The Copper Damascene Process

The most critical industrial application: the damascene processNamed after ancient metal inlaying art from Damascus, Syria. Copper is electroplated into pre-etched trenches in dielectric, then excess is polished away by CMP—an additive metallization approach. for copper interconnects.

Why Copper Replaced Aluminum

PropertyAluminumCopperAdvantage
Resistivity (μΩ·cm)2.651.68Cu: 37% lower
ElectromigrationLower resistanceHigher resistanceCu wires last longer
Thermal conductivity237 W/m·K401 W/m·KCu dissipates heat better

Dual Damascene Process Steps

Deposit & Pattern Dielectric

Low-k dielectric deposited and patterned via lithography + etching to create trenches and vias.

Barrier Layer (Ta/TaN)

PVD-deposited barrier prevents copper diffusion into silicon that would destroy transistors.

Copper Seed Layer

Thin (~50–150 nm) PVD copper provides the conductive surface for electroplating.

Electroplate Copper

Wafer immersed in CuSO&sub4; bath with additives (accelerators, suppressors, levelers) enabling bottom-up superfilling of features.

CMP Planarization

Chemical-mechanical polishing removes excess copper, leaving perfectly inlaid wiring in trenches.

🔑 Superfilling

The “magic” of damascene: bath additives make deposition faster at trench bottoms than tops, enabling void-free filling of 20 nm features—a remarkable electrochemical engineering achievement.

📝 Experimental Procedure

Part A: Gold Electrodeposition

Prepare Electrolyte

Pour ~30 mL gold(III) chloride solution into a clean beaker. Solution should appear pale yellow and cover at least 2 cm of immersed electrode.

Assemble Cell

Connect negative terminal → FTO glass (cathode). Connect positive terminal → graphite (anode). Ensure FTO conductive side faces the solution (test with multimeter).

Deposition #1: 10 min

Immerse electrodes, apply voltage, start timer. Observe color changes. After 10 min, remove, rinse with DI water. Record color.

Deposition #2: 10 min (replicate)

Fresh FTO slide, same conditions, 10 min. This sample will be used for oxidation. Record color.

Deposition #3: ~1 hour

Third FTO slide, extended deposition. Watch progressive color changes. Record final color.

Part B: Oxidation

Electrical Oxidation

Sample #1 in 1.0 M KCl. Connect positive lead to gold film (now the anode). Apply voltage 5–10 min. Record color changes.

Thermal Oxidation

Place samples #2 and #3 on hot plate (~150–200°C) for 10–15 min. Record before/after colors.
